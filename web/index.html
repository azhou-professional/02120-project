1. The idea in one paragraph

A GPS receiver does not measure its position directly. It measures the travel time of a signal from each satellite, which gives a distance, the pseudorange. The receiver's own clock is cheap and wrong by an unknown amount \(\delta t\), and light covers 300 km in a millisecond, so the clock error \(b = c\,\delta t\) (written as a distance) is a fourth unknown next to \(x, y, z\):

\[ \rho_i = \lVert \mathbf{s}_i - \mathbf{r} \rVert + b + \varepsilon_i , \qquad i = 1, \dots, N \]

where \(\mathbf{s}_i\) is satellite \(i\)'s position, \(\mathbf{r}\) the receiver's position and \(\varepsilon_i\) the measurement error. Four unknowns need at least four satellites. How much the errors \(\varepsilon_i\) are magnified into a position error depends only on the directions to the satellites. That magnification is the dilution of precision (DOP). The simulator finds where the satellites are (section 2), which ones the receiver can see (section 3), computes the DOP (section 4), and repeats this at random places and times (Monte Carlo) to measure coverage, which is checked against an exact formula (section 5).

2. Orbits: where the satellites are

Each satellite follows a two-body Kepler orbit around a point-mass Earth, with \(\mu = GM_\oplus = 398\,600.4418\ \text{km}^3/\text{s}^2\). An orbit is described by six elements:

ElementSymbolMeaning
semi-major axis\(a\)size of the ellipse (the radius for a circle)
eccentricity\(e\)shape: 0 = circle, close to 1 = long and thin
inclination\(i\)tilt of the orbit plane from the equator
RAAN\(\Omega\)where the orbit crosses the equator going north, measured from \(+x\)
argument of perigee\(\omega\)where the closest point lies within the plane
mean anomaly at \(t=0\)\(M_0\)where the satellite is along the orbit at the start

Period and mean motion (Kepler's third law)

\[ n = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{a^3}}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{n} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}} , \qquad M(t) = M_0 + n\,t \]

constellation.mean_motion, constellation.period. The mean anomaly \(M\) grows at a steady rate. It is the angle the satellite would have if it moved at constant speed.

Kepler's equation

On an ellipse the satellite speeds up near perigee, so its real angle is not \(M\). The eccentric anomaly \(E\) links the two:

\[ M = E - e \sin E \]

There is no closed-form solution for \(E\), so it is solved with Newton's method. With \(f(E) = E - e\sin E - M\) and \(f'(E) = 1 - e\cos E\):

\[ E_{k+1} = E_k - \frac{E_k - e\sin E_k - M}{1 - e\cos E_k}, \qquad E_0 = M + 0.85\,e\,\operatorname{sign}(\sin M) \]

constellation.solve_kepler (stops when the step is below \(10^{-13}\) rad). Since \(f'(E) \ge 1 - e > 0\), the step never divides by zero for \(e < 1\). Worked example D below.

Position in the orbit plane, then in space

In the orbit plane (Earth's centre at one focus, \(x\) towards perigee):

\[ x_{\text{pf}} = a(\cos E - e), \qquad y_{\text{pf}} = a\sqrt{1-e^2}\,\sin E, \qquad r = a(1 - e\cos E) \]

Three rotations carry the point into the inertial frame (\(+z\) through the north pole, \(+x\) towards the vernal equinox): first by \(\omega\) about the orbit normal, then by \(i\) about the line of nodes, then by \(\Omega\) about the pole:

\[ \mathbf{s} = R_z(\Omega)\, R_x(i)\, R_z(\omega) \begin{pmatrix} x_{\text{pf}} \\ y_{\text{pf}} \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \]

constellation.positions, constellation._to_inertial. For a circle (\(e = 0\)) this reduces to \(a(\cos M, \sin M)\) in the plane, which a test checks. Speed comes from the vis-viva equation \(v^2 = \mu\,(2/r - 1/a)\) (constellation.speed).

Walker constellations

A Walker pattern \(T/P/F\) places \(T\) satellites in \(P\) equally spaced circular planes. Plane \(p\) (\(p = 0 \dots P-1\)) and slot \(s\) (\(s = 0 \dots T/P - 1\)) get

\[ \Omega_p = \frac{360^\circ\, p}{P}, \qquad M_{0} = \frac{360^\circ\, s}{T/P} + \frac{360^\circ\, F\, p}{T} \]

constellation.walker. The idealized GPS is Walker 24/6/1 at \(a = 26\,559.7\) km, \(i = 55^\circ\).

3. Earth and sky: what the receiver sees

The rotating Earth

The satellites move in the inertial frame while the Earth turns underneath them once per sidereal day (86 164.0905 s, which is 4 minutes shorter than a solar day because the Earth also moves around the Sun). The angle from \(+x\) to Greenwich is the Greenwich Mean Sidereal Time (Meeus, eq. 12.4):

\[ \theta(t) = \theta_0 + \omega_\oplus t, \qquad \omega_\oplus = \frac{2\pi}{86\,164.0905\ \text{s}}, \qquad \theta_0 = \text{GMST}(\text{2026-01-01 00:00 UTC}) \approx 100.66^\circ \]

geometry.gmst_deg, geometry.earth_angle_deg.

Receiver position and its local East-North-Up frame

For latitude \(\varphi\) and longitude \(\lambda\), the inertial longitude is \(\lambda' = \lambda + \theta(t)\), and on a sphere of radius \(R = 6378.137\) km:

\[ \mathbf{r} = R \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi\cos\lambda' \\ \cos\varphi\sin\lambda' \\ \sin\varphi \end{pmatrix}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{E}} = \begin{pmatrix} -\sin\lambda' \\ \cos\lambda' \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{N}} = \begin{pmatrix} -\sin\varphi\cos\lambda' \\ -\sin\varphi\sin\lambda' \\ \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{U}} = \frac{\mathbf{r}}{R} \]

geometry.receiver_position, geometry.enu_basis.

Elevation and azimuth

The unit vector from the receiver to satellite \(i\) is \(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i = (\mathbf{s}_i - \mathbf{r}) / \lVert \mathbf{s}_i - \mathbf{r}\rVert\). Then

\[ \text{el}_i = \arcsin\big(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i\cdot\hat{\mathbf{U}}\big), \qquad \text{az}_i = \operatorname{atan2}\big(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i\cdot\hat{\mathbf{E}},\ \hat{\mathbf{e}}_i\cdot\hat{\mathbf{N}}\big) \]

geometry.look_angles. A satellite counts as visible if it is switched on and \(\text{el}_i \ge\) the blocking angle (0° in the open, or the valley's wall angle in the Terrain tab, geometry.blocking_angle).

4. Dilution of precision (DOP)

Linearizing the pseudorange equations

Start from a guess \(\mathbf{r}_0, b_0\) and ask how the pseudoranges change for a small change \(\delta\mathbf{r}, \delta b\). The derivative of a distance \(\lVert\mathbf{s}_i - \mathbf{r}\rVert\) with respect to \(\mathbf{r}\) is \(-\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i\), so

\[ \delta\rho_i = -\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i\cdot\delta\mathbf{r} + \delta b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \delta\boldsymbol{\rho} = G\,\delta\mathbf{x}, \qquad G = \begin{pmatrix} -e_{1x} & -e_{1y} & -e_{1z} & 1 \\ -e_{2x} & -e_{2y} & -e_{2z} & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ -e_{Nx} & -e_{Ny} & -e_{Nz} & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \delta\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} \delta x \\ \delta y \\ \delta z \\ \delta b \end{pmatrix} \]

\(G\) is the geometry matrix. It has one row per visible satellite and depends only on directions.

Least squares, and where DOP comes from

With \(N \ge 4\) satellites the least-squares solution is \(\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (G^{\mathsf T}G)^{-1}G^{\mathsf T}\delta\boldsymbol{\rho}\). If every pseudorange has an independent error of the same size \(\sigma\), the covariance of the solution is

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\delta\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \sigma^2\,(G^{\mathsf T}G)^{-1} = \sigma^2 Q, \qquad Q = (G^{\mathsf T}G)^{-1} \ \ (4\times4) \]

So \(Q\) says how much the geometry multiplies the range error. The DOPs are square roots of sums of its diagonal elements:

\[ \text{GDOP} = \sqrt{Q_{xx}+Q_{yy}+Q_{zz}+Q_{bb}}, \quad \text{PDOP} = \sqrt{Q_{xx}+Q_{yy}+Q_{zz}}, \quad \text{TDOP} = \sqrt{Q_{bb}} \]

For horizontal and vertical, rotate the 3×3 position block into East-North-Up with \(R_{\text{ENU}} = \big(\hat{\mathbf{E}}\ \hat{\mathbf{N}}\ \hat{\mathbf{U}}\big)^{\mathsf T}\):

\[ Q_{\text{ENU}} = R_{\text{ENU}}\, Q_{3\times3}\, R_{\text{ENU}}^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \text{HDOP} = \sqrt{Q_{EE} + Q_{NN}}, \qquad \text{VDOP} = \sqrt{Q_{UU}} \]

dop.dop (one receiver) and dop.dop_batch (many Monte Carlo trials at once). Rotation does not change a trace, so \(\text{PDOP}^2 = \text{HDOP}^2 + \text{VDOP}^2\) and \(\text{GDOP}^2 = \text{PDOP}^2 + \text{TDOP}^2\). Both identities are tested.

In words: 1-sigma position error ≈ PDOP × range error. With 1 m range errors and PDOP = 2, the 3-D position is uncertain by about 2 m. Small DOP means good geometry. "Good positioning" in this app means at least 4 satellites and PDOP ≤ 6 (editable).

Why four satellites, and when it fails anyway

  • \(G\) has \(N\) rows, so \(G^{\mathsf T}G\) has rank at most \(N\). With \(N < 4\) it cannot be inverted, so there are more unknowns than equations.
  • Even with many satellites, \(G^{\mathsf T}G\) is singular if all the directions lie on one cone around some axis \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}\), i.e. \(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i\cdot\hat{\mathbf{a}} = k\) for every \(i\). Then \(G\,(\hat{\mathbf{a}}, k)^{\mathsf T} = \mathbf{0}\): moving along \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}\) and changing the clock by \(k\) changes no pseudorange. Satellites all on the horizon (\(\hat{\mathbf{a}} = \hat{\mathbf{U}}, k = 0\)) are a special case. The code returns NaN when the condition number of \(G^{\mathsf T}G\) exceeds \(10^{12}\).
  • Adding a satellite adds a positive term \(\mathbf{g}\mathbf{g}^{\mathsf T}\) to \(G^{\mathsf T}G\), so the DOP can only go down or stay the same (tested).

5. The analytic coverage check

One satellite at radius \(a\) is above elevation \(\varepsilon\) for every point within a central angle \(\lambda\) of the point directly below it. In the triangle Earth centre–receiver–satellite, the angle at the receiver is \(90^\circ + \varepsilon\). The law of sines gives the angle \(\eta\) at the satellite: \(\sin\eta = R\cos\varepsilon / a\). The three angles add up to \(180^\circ\), so

\[ \lambda = 90^\circ - \varepsilon - \eta = \arccos\!\left(\frac{R\cos\varepsilon}{a}\right) - \varepsilon \]

That region is a spherical cap whose area is \(2\pi R^2(1-\cos\lambda)\). Dividing by the sphere's area \(4\pi R^2\) gives the probability that a uniformly random point sees the satellite:

\[ P = \frac{1 - \cos\lambda}{2}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\text{number in view}] = \sum_{\text{satellites}} P_i \]

montecarlo.cap_half_angle, montecarlo.single_sat_probability, montecarlo.expected_mean_visible. The sum works even though visibilities are correlated, because the expectation of a sum is always the sum of the expectations. For an ellipse \(r\) changes, so \(P\) is averaged over the orbit. The Monte Carlo picks \(\sin\varphi\) uniform in \([-1,1]\) and \(\lambda\) uniform in \([0^\circ, 360^\circ)\), which is uniform by area. Its mean must match this value within its statistical error. This is the check shown in the Monte Carlo tab.

6. Worked examples

A. The ideal four-satellite geometry: a regular tetrahedron

Put one satellite at the zenith and three at elevation \(\arcsin(-\tfrac13) = -19.47^\circ\), 120° apart in azimuth. The four unit vectors point to the corners of a regular tetrahedron. In East-North-Up coordinates, with \(s = \sqrt{8}/3 = 0.9428\):

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}_1 = (0, 0, 1), \qquad \hat{\mathbf{e}}_{2,3,4} = \big(s\cos\alpha,\ s\sin\alpha,\ -\tfrac13\big),\ \ \alpha = 0^\circ, 120^\circ, 240^\circ \] \[ G = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & -1 & 1 \\ -0.9428 & 0 & 0.3333 & 1 \\ 0.4714 & -0.8165 & 0.3333 & 1 \\ 0.4714 & 0.8165 & 0.3333 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

Build \(G^{\mathsf T}G\) one block at a time:

  • Position block \(\sum_i \hat{\mathbf{e}}_i\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i^{\mathsf T}\): by symmetry it is a multiple of the identity. The \(zz\) entry is \(1 + 3\cdot\tfrac19 = \tfrac43\). The \(xx\) entry is \(s^2(1 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14) = \tfrac89\cdot\tfrac32 = \tfrac43\).
  • Position–clock entries \(-\sum_i \hat{\mathbf{e}}_i = \mathbf{0}\), because the corners of a regular tetrahedron add up to zero.
  • Clock–clock entry: \(\sum_i 1 = N = 4\).
\[ G^{\mathsf T}G = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac43 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \tfrac43 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac43 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac34 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \tfrac34 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac34 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \tfrac14 \end{pmatrix} \] \[ \text{GDOP} = \sqrt{\tfrac94 + \tfrac14} = \tfrac{\sqrt{10}}{2}, \quad \text{PDOP} = \sqrt{\tfrac94} = \tfrac32, \quad \text{HDOP} = \sqrt{\tfrac32}, \quad \text{VDOP} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \quad \text{TDOP} = \tfrac12 \]
Result: GDOP 1.5811, PDOP 1.5000, HDOP 1.2247, VDOP 0.8660, TDOP 0.5000. Langley (1999) gives GDOP 1.581, the smallest possible with four satellites. Three of the satellites are below the horizon, so a receiver on the ground can never actually have this geometry, but the matrix algebra does not care.

B. The GPS orbit period, and how many satellites are in view

Period. With \(a = 26\,559.7\) km and \(\mu = 398\,600.4418\ \text{km}^3/\text{s}^2\):

\[ a^3 = 1.87357\times10^{13}\ \text{km}^3, \quad \frac{a^3}{\mu} = 4.70037\times10^{7}\ \text{s}^2, \quad \sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}} = 6855.92\ \text{s}, \quad T = 2\pi \times 6855.92 = 43\,077.0\ \text{s} \]

That is 11 h 57 min 57 s, just 5 s short of half a sidereal day (43 082.0 s). A GPS satellite therefore makes two orbits while the Earth turns once, and its ground track repeats every day, 4 minutes earlier each solar day. Its speed is \(v = \sqrt{\mu/a} = 3.874\) km/s.

One satellite's coverage, mask 0°. With \(\varepsilon = 0\) the formula of section 5 becomes \(\cos\lambda = R/a\):

\[ \frac{R}{a} = \frac{6378.137}{26\,559.7} = 0.240143, \quad \lambda = \arccos(0.240143) = 76.105^\circ, \quad P = \frac{1 - 0.240143}{2} = 0.379928 \]

All 24 satellites: \(24 \times 0.379928 = 9.118\). Mask 5°: \(R\cos 5^\circ/a = 0.239230\), \(\lambda = 76.159^\circ - 5^\circ = 71.159^\circ\), \(P = (1 - \cos 71.159^\circ)/2 = 0.338528\), and \(24P = 8.125\). Raising the mask by 5° costs about one satellite on average.

Result: \(T\) = 43,077.0 s; mask 0°: \(\lambda\) = 76.105°, \(P\) = 0.37993, expected number in view 9.118; mask 5°: \(\lambda\) = 71.159°, \(P\) = 0.33853, expected number in view 8.125. The Monte Carlo tab's mean number in view should land within its error bar of 9.118 (open sky).

C. Langley's example: why the clock costs you height

One satellite at the zenith and three at elevation 15°, at azimuths 0°, 120° and 240°. With \(\sin15^\circ = 0.258819\) and \(\cos15^\circ = 0.965926\), the East-North-Up directions are

\[ \hat{\mathbf{e}}_1 = (0, 0, 1),\quad \hat{\mathbf{e}}_2 = (0, 0.9659, 0.2588),\quad \hat{\mathbf{e}}_3 = (0.8365, -0.4830, 0.2588),\quad \hat{\mathbf{e}}_4 = (-0.8365, -0.4830, 0.2588) \]

Sum the products column by column. The horizontal entries are \(\tfrac32\cos^2 15^\circ = 1.399519\). The up entry is \(1 + 3\sin^2 15^\circ = 1.200962\). The up–clock entry is \(-(1 + 3\sin15^\circ) = -1.776457\). The clock entry is 4. Everything else is zero by symmetry:

\[ G^{\mathsf T}G = \begin{pmatrix} 1.399519 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1.399519 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1.200962 & -1.776457 \\ 0 & 0 & -1.776457 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \]

The horizontal part inverts entry by entry: \(Q_{EE} = Q_{NN} = 1/1.399519 = 0.714531\). The up–clock part is a 2×2 block, \(\begin{pmatrix} p & q \\ q & r \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{pr - q^2}\begin{pmatrix} r & -q \\ -q & p \end{pmatrix}\), with determinant \(1.200962 \times 4 - 1.776457^2 = 1.648048\):

\[ Q_{UU} = \frac{4}{1.648048} = 2.427114, \qquad Q_{bb} = \frac{1.200962}{1.648048} = 0.728718, \qquad Q_{Ub} = \frac{1.776457}{1.648048} = 1.077916 \]

HDOP \(= \sqrt{2 \times 0.714531}\) and VDOP \(= \sqrt{2.427114}\). Now suppose the receiver had a perfect clock. The fourth column of \(G\) disappears, the up entry is no longer tied to the clock, and \(Q_{UU} = 1/1.200962 = 0.832666\).

Result: HDOP 1.1954, VDOP 1.5579, TDOP 0.8536, PDOP 1.9637, GDOP 2.1412. With the clock known: HDOP 1.1954 (unchanged), VDOP 0.9125. Langley (1999) prints 1.195, 1.558 and 0.913. The Compare tab shows the same check.
Why: every satellite is above the horizon, so every \(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i\) has a positive up component. Moving the receiver up shortens every pseudorange, and a clock running early also shortens every pseudorange. The two effects look alike, which shows up as the large off-diagonal \(Q_{Ub}\). Separating them costs vertical precision. Horizontal errors are not affected, because east and north are not correlated with the clock. This is why GPS is worse at height than at horizontal position.

7. Limitations, and why these choices were made

Every model leaves things out. Each entry says what is simplified, why that choice was made, and how much it matters for the results. The labels A1–A21 refer to the assumptions table in the README, which is kept up to date.

The Earth

L1. The Earth is a perfect sphere (A1)

Simplified
A sphere of radius 6378.137 km (the WGS-84 equatorial radius) instead of the WGS-84 ellipsoid, whose polar radius is 21.4 km smaller.
Why
"Up" is then simply \(\mathbf{r}/R\), and the coverage formula of section 5 is exact, which gives an analytic check.
Effect
The sphere's "up" differs from the ellipsoid's by at most 0.19° (at 45° latitude), so elevations are off by at most about 0.2°. This is negligible for DOP, and may contribute a little to the 0.01–0.05 differences from the FAA's 99.9% PDOP.

L2. Simplified Earth rotation and time (A2)

Simplified
A constant rotation rate (one turn per 86 164.0905 s). No precession, nutation or polar motion, and UTC is used in place of UT1.
Why
These effects change the Earth's orientation by at most tens of arcseconds over a year. Modelling them needs Earth-orientation data tables and would not change any coverage or DOP statistic.
Effect
UT1 − UTC is under 0.9 s, about 0.004° of rotation. The Monte Carlo statistics do not depend on the Earth's angle at all, because the receiver's inertial longitude is uniform anyway (A11).

The satellites

L3. Two-body Kepler orbits, no perturbations (A3)

Simplified
No Earth oblateness (J2), atmospheric drag, Sun and Moon gravity, solar radiation pressure or station-keeping manoeuvres.
Why
Kepler motion has an exact solution that can be tested (period, energy and angular momentum conservation, the two-focus property). Perturbations at GPS altitude are slow, and the question is about geometry, not precise orbit prediction.
Effect
J2 turns the GPS orbit planes by about −0.04°/day, roughly 14° a year. All the planes turn together, so the shape of the constellation and the DOP statistics barely change. Absolute positions drift: the 1999 almanac preset drifts by about 18 km/day from where the almanac puts the satellites (A19).

L4. Idealized, always-healthy constellations (A4, A14)

Simplified
GPS is an ideal Walker 24/6/1, not the real slot assignments. Galileo and BeiDou use their nominal designs. Spares are ignored and every satellite always works.
Why
It is a clean, reproducible baseline, and it is the reference design that the published standards are built around. The real constellation changes from month to month.
Effect
It is optimistic. With the FAA method, the ideal Walker 24/6/1 has a global 99.9% PDOP of 3.14, while the real 1999 constellation (27 satellites!) had 3.21–5.60 depending on the week. Evenly spaced slots matter more than extra satellites. The Compare tab uses the real almanacs for exactly this reason.

L5. Failures are chosen by hand (A16)

Simplified
Satellites switched off in the Constellation tab count as failed. There is no random outage model.
Why
It shows directly how one specific failure degrades coverage. A probabilistic model would need outage-rate data that this project does not have.
Effect
The availability shown is for that set of satellites, not an expected availability averaged over typical outages.

The receiver and its sky

L6. A fixed receiver on the surface (A5)

Simplified
Height 0, not moving. No antenna pattern and no multipath.
Why
The question is about a user on the surface of the Earth.
Effect
An aircraft or a mountain top sees slightly below the geometric horizon, which this model leaves out.

L7. Purely geometric visibility (A6)

Simplified
A satellite is usable if its elevation is at or above the blocking angle. No atmospheric refraction and no signal-strength limit. The default mask is 0°.
Why
An elevation mask is the standard way to define visibility in coverage studies (the FAA uses 5°). It also keeps the analytic check exact.
Effect
Refraction lifts a satellite's apparent elevation by about 0.5° at the horizon, which is small. Real receivers often ignore satellites below 5–15° because their signals are noisy. Example B shows that a 5° mask lowers the mean number in view from 9.118 to 8.125.

L8. Terrain is a cone-shaped valley (A18)

Simplified
The walls rise to the same angle in every direction. Blocked satellites are fully hidden: no diffraction, no partial blocking and no reflections.
Why
A cone behaves exactly like an elevation mask, so the analytic check still holds. The code has a hook (geometry.blocking_angle) for direction-dependent shapes.
Effect
A real street or valley blocks some directions more than others (a street canyon may leave satellites only along its length), which can make DOP much worse than the average wall height suggests. Multipath, often the largest error in cities, is not captured. For GPS alone, availability falls from 100% in the open to 99.6% at 10°, 87.9% at 20°, 34.4% at 30° and 1.8% at 40°.

The error model

L9. Only geometry, with all satellites weighted equally (A7, A9)

Simplified
DOP uses only the directions to the satellites. There is no ranging-error model (ionosphere, troposphere, satellite clock and orbit errors), and every satellite gets the same weight.
Why
DOP separates the geometry, which is the scientific question here, from the signal errors, which would need atmosphere and clock models. Position error ≈ DOP × range error, so a DOP result holds for any error budget.
Effect
The app gives no position error in metres. Real low-elevation signals pass through more atmosphere and are noisier. Weighting them equally makes low satellites look as useful as high ones, which is optimistic.

L10. One receiver clock for all constellations (A8)

Simplified
The unknowns are always \(x, y, z, b\), even when GPS, Galileo and BeiDou are mixed.
Why
This keeps the 4-column \(G\) of section 4. Real receivers either use broadcast time offsets between the systems or solve for one extra clock unknown per extra system.
Effect
Optimistic for mixed constellations, most of all when few satellites are visible (deep valleys). It has no effect for GPS alone.

L11. "Good positioning" means PDOP ≤ 6 (A10)

Simplified
A fix counts as good if at least 4 satellites are in view and PDOP ≤ 6.
Why
It is the limit used in the GPS SPS performance standard and the FAA reports, so the results can be compared with them.
Effect
It is a convention, not physics. The threshold can be edited, and availability depends on it.

The statistics

L12. Monte Carlo sampling and its error bar (A11, A12)

Simplified
Independent random places (uniform by area) and times (uniform over a window). The 95% interval for availability is the normal approximation \(\pm1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). At most 1,000,000 trials.
Why
It is the textbook binomial error and easy to check by hand. The trial limit keeps a run practical in the browser.
Effect
The normal approximation is too narrow when \(p\) is very close to 0 or 1. If every trial is good, it gives an interval of width 0, which does not mean availability is exactly 100%. Read intervals for availabilities like 99.99% with care. A Wilson interval would fix this. A time window that is not a whole number of repeat cycles weights part of a cycle more, by a few percent at most.

The comparison with published data

L13. Reproducing the FAA report needs some inferences (A19, A20)

Simplified
The FAA report does not say which day of each week it used, which satellites it counted as healthy, or exactly how "global 99.9% PDOP" is defined. We use the 24 h from each almanac's reference time, and a definition that reproduces its values.
Why
These are the only choices the published information allows. Each one is stated, not hidden.
Effect
10 of 14 weeks match with no adjustment, and 3 match once the satellite set is inferred. Week 1007 remains unexplained, and that is reported rather than forced (Compare tab, "?" marks).

Only for the picture

L14. Sun, Moon, Earth colours and satellite models (A21)

Simplified
Low-precision Sun and Moon positions, no eclipses, no Earth shadow, stylized land colours, and satellite models drawn far larger than life.
Why
They make the scene easier to read.
Effect
None. No DOP, coverage or Monte Carlo result uses them.
